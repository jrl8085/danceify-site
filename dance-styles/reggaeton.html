About Reggaeton Dance

Reggaeton emerged in the housing projects of Puerto Rico during the early 1990s, a musical and cultural movement born from the collision of Jamaican dancehall, Latin American hip-hop, and Caribbean electronic music. The genre's rhythmic foundation is the dembow riddim — a syncopated beat pattern originally sampled from Shabba Ranks' 1990 dancehall track "Dem Bow" — which provides the unmistakable boom-ch-boom-chick that drives nearly every reggaeton song ever made.

The dance that evolved alongside this music is visceral and body-driven. Perreo, the most iconic reggaeton dance style, emphasizes hip movement, low stances, and rhythmic body rolls synchronized to the dembow beat. Unlike formal partner dances with prescribed steps, perreo is improvisational and responsive — dancers react to the music's energy shifts, dropping low during bass-heavy drops and riding the rhythm with undulating torso movements during melodic passages.

As reggaeton conquered global charts through artists like Daddy Yankee, Bad Bunny, J Balvin, and Karol G, its dance vocabulary expanded. Modern reggaeton choreography incorporates elements from hip-hop isolations, Afro-Caribbean body waves, and even TikTok-era viral moves. The style has become a lingua franca of Latin nightlife worldwide — from San Juan to Medellín to Madrid to Miami, the dembow beat hits and everyone knows how to move.
