About Salsa Dance

Salsa was forged in the dance halls of 1960s and 1970s New York City, where Cuban son, mambo, and Puerto Rican bomba collided with jazz and funk in the barrios of Spanish Harlem and the Bronx. The term "salsa" — literally "sauce" — was popularized by Fania Records and its roster of musicians who blended these traditions into something urgent and new. The dance that emerged alongside the music borrowed the structured partner framework of Cuban casino rueda while adding the linear footwork and flashy turn patterns favored by New York dancers.

At its core, salsa is danced on a quick-quick-slow rhythm pattern timed to the clave, the five-stroke rhythmic backbone of Afro-Cuban music. Dancers shift their weight across three beats, pause on the fourth, and repeat. This produces the rolling hip motion called Cuban motion — not a deliberate hip shake, but the natural consequence of proper weight transfer through bent and straightened knees. The upper body stays relatively still while the lower body does the talking.

Two dominant lineages exist today. LA-style salsa (salsa on 1) favors dramatic dips, aerials, and showy performance. New York-style (salsa on 2) emphasizes smooth musicality and connection, breaking on the second beat for a more grounded, jazz-influenced feel. Both share the same foundational steps but differ in timing, attitude, and emphasis.
