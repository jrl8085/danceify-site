About Hula

Hula is far more than a dance — it is the living archive of Hawaiian culture, a tradition through which history, mythology, genealogy, and spiritual beliefs have been preserved and transmitted across generations in a culture that had no written language until the nineteenth century. Every hula tells a story. The hand gestures represent specific words and concepts — rain, ocean, love, mountain, wind — while the footwork and hip movement provide the rhythmic foundation that carries the narrative forward.

Two major forms exist. Hula kahiko (ancient hula) is the older tradition, performed to chanting (oli) and percussion instruments like the ipu (gourd drum), the pūniu (coconut shell drum), and the ‘ili‘ili (river stones used as castanets). Kahiko is powerful and grounded, with strong stamps, deep knee bends, and an intensity that connects the dancer to the earth and to ancestral spirits. Hula ‘auana (modern hula) emerged in the nineteenth century after Christian missionaries suppressed the practice. When King David Kalākaua revived hula in the 1880s, it absorbed Western musical influences — ukulele, slack-key guitar, steel guitar — and developed a softer, more flowing style.

The foundation of all hula is the vamp — a basic stepping pattern performed with bent knees that keeps the dancer low to the ground. The hips sway naturally as a consequence of the weight shifts, not as a separate embellishment. Above the waist, the hands and arms create the hula hands — flowing, precise gestures that illustrate the mele (song or chant). A hand waving from side to side might represent ocean waves; fingers fluttering downward might signify rain; arms extended outward could indicate a mountain range.
