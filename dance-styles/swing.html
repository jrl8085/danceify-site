About Swing Dance

Swing dance was born in Harlem during the late 1920s and early 1930s, a direct physical response to the swinging jazz music that was revolutionizing American popular culture. The Lindy Hop — named, according to legend, after Charles Lindbergh's transatlantic "hop" — emerged at the Savoy Ballroom, a racially integrated dance hall on Lenox Avenue where some of the era's most innovative social dancers tested new moves against the music of bands led by Chick Webb, Count Basie, and Benny Goodman.

What made swing revolutionary was its breakaway — a moment where partners separated from closed position into open position, creating space for individual improvisation. This simple structural innovation opened a universe of possibilities. Dancers could swing out, add solo jazz steps, attempt aerial moves, and reconnect, all within the elastic framework of the basic swing rhythm. The dance breathed with the music, stretching and compressing in response to the band's dynamics.

Swing encompasses several related styles. Lindy Hop is the original, characterized by its eight-count basic, athletic aerials, and deep connection to jazz music. East Coast Swing simplified Lindy into a six-count pattern more accessible to casual social dancers. West Coast Swing evolved on the West Coast with a slotted, smoother style suited to blues and R&B. Jive sped things up into a high-energy competition form. All share the same DNA: a bouncy, grounded connection to the music's swing feel.
