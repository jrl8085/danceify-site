About Irish Step Dance

Irish step dance is one of the most visually distinctive dance forms in the world, instantly recognizable by its defining paradox: extraordinary speed and complexity below the waist paired with an almost completely still upper body. Arms held rigid at the sides, back ramrod straight, chin level — while below, the feet execute intricate patterns of clicks, stamps, hops, and aerial movements at speeds that can exceed ten beats per second.

The origins of Irish step dance are debated, but most historians trace its development to the eighteenth-century dancing masters who traveled between towns in rural Ireland, teaching steps and creating new dances for local communities. These itinerant teachers competed with each other for students, driving innovation in footwork complexity. The rigid upper body is often attributed to the cramped spaces where lessons took place — kitchen tables, barn doors, sometimes literally dancing on a door laid flat — but it likely also reflects the formal deportment expectations of the era.

Two shoe types create two fundamentally different sounds and styles. Soft shoes (ghillies for women, reel shoes for men) produce a whisper-quiet dance where the emphasis is on elevation, pointed toes, and the visual beauty of crossing and uncrossing the feet. Hard shoes (heavy shoes with fiberglass tips and heels) transform the dancer into a percussionist, producing thunderous rhythmic patterns called trebles — rapid-fire beats created by striking the toe and heel in alternation. The hard shoe treble is to Irish dance what the zapateado is to flamenco: the style's rhythmic signature.
