About Ballet

Ballet is the foundational discipline of Western theatrical dance, with roots stretching back to the Italian Renaissance courts of the fifteenth century. What began as elaborate court entertainments — performances staged for royalty featuring poetry, music, and movement — was formalized into a codified technique during the reign of France's Louis XIV. The Sun King established the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661, creating the institutional framework that transformed ballet from courtly amusement into a professional art form with a standardized vocabulary that persists to this day.

Every ballet movement has a French name, and every student learns the same foundational positions. The five positions of the feet. The plié — a controlled bend of the knees. The tendu — a sliding extension of the foot along the floor. The arabesque — balancing on one leg while the other extends behind the body. These building blocks combine into phrases of movement that prioritize line, turnout, and the illusion of effortlessness.

What makes ballet visually distinctive is its pursuit of vertical space. Dancers work against gravity: rising onto pointe (the tips of specially reinforced shoes), executing grand jetés that seem to suspend them in midair, and holding extensions at heights that demand extraordinary flexibility and strength. The upper body — the épaulement — coordinates head, shoulders, and arms into positions that frame the movement and give it emotional direction.
